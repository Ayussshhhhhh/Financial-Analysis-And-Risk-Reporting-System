## P&L Metrics

---

### 1. Revenue

**Business Definition:** The total income generated by the lending portfolio from all operating sources during a reporting period, before any cost deductions. It represents the top line of the income statement and is the starting point for all profitability calculations.

**Formula:**
```
Revenue = Interest Income + Origination Fee Income + Late Fee Income
        + Prepayment Fee Income + Other Operating Income
```

**Data Source Fields:** `gl_account_category`, `gl_amount`, `gl_period`, `income_type`

**Grain:** Monthly (summed across all income account codes for the period)

**Filter Rules:**
- Include only rows where `gl_account_category IN ('Interest Income', 'Fee Income', 'Other Operating Income')`.
- Exclude intercompany eliminations (`is_intercompany = TRUE`).
- Exclude reversals posted in a future period (`reversal_period IS NOT NULL AND reversal_period > gl_period`).
- Restrict to `gl_status = 'Posted'` (exclude draft and provisional entries).

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly (final, 5 business days after close); preliminary estimate available on day 2.

**Owner:** Finance — Management Accounting

**Known Limitations:**
- Interest income is recognised on an accrual basis, which may differ from cash receipts in periods with significant payment delays. Cash-basis reconciliation is a manual step performed by Treasury.
- Origination fees on multi-year loans are amortised using the effective interest rate method, so reported fee income may differ from cash collected in any given month.

**Example:**  
Period: October 2024  
Interest Income: \$4,200,000  
Origination Fee Income: \$380,000  
Late Fee Income: \$95,000  
Prepayment Fee Income: \$42,000  
**Revenue = \$4,717,000**

### 2. Gross Profit

**Business Definition:** The income remaining after deducting the direct costs of generating the lending revenue—primarily the provision for credit losses and the cost of funds (interest expense on borrowings used to fund the loan book). It measures the fundamental profitability of the core lending activity before overhead costs are considered.

**Formula:**
```
Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

Where COGS = Provision for Credit Losses + Interest Expense (Cost of Funds)
           + Direct Loan Servicing Costs
```

**Data Source Fields:** `gl_account_category`, `gl_amount`, `gl_period`, `cost_category`

**Grain:** Monthly

**Filter Rules:**
- COGS includes only direct cost accounts: `gl_account_category IN ('Provision for Credit Losses', 'Interest Expense', 'Loan Servicing Cost')`.
- Operating overhead (salaries, rent, technology) is excluded from COGS and reported under OpEx.
- Apply the same `gl_status = 'Posted'` and intercompany filters as Revenue.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Finance — Management Accounting

**Known Limitations:**
- The allocation of shared loan-servicing costs between COGS and OpEx requires a manual cost-driver model that is updated quarterly. Changes to the allocation methodology between quarters may cause apparent period-over-period shifts that are methodological rather than operational.

**Example:**  
Revenue: \$4,717,000  
Provision for Credit Losses: \$620,000  
Interest Expense: \$890,000  
Loan Servicing Costs: \$145,000  
COGS = \$1,655,000  
**Gross Profit = \$4,717,000 − \$1,655,000 = \$3,062,000**

---

### 3. Gross Margin %

**Business Definition:** The proportion of Revenue that remains as Gross Profit. It measures how efficiently the business converts its top-line income into profit after direct costs, and is a key indicator of the core lending business's unit economics.

**Formula:**
```
Gross Margin % = (Gross Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100
```

**Data Source Fields:** Derived from Revenue and Gross Profit (see metrics 1 and 2).

**Grain:** Monthly

**Filter Rules:**
- If Revenue = 0 for a period, Gross Margin % is undefined; display as `N/A` rather than zero or infinity.
- Calculated at entity level and also rolled up to portfolio level.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Finance — Management Accounting

**Known Limitations:**
- Gross Margin % is sensitive to the COGS/OpEx boundary. If reclassifications occur (e.g., servicing costs moved from COGS to OpEx), the ratio changes without an underlying business change. Reclassifications must be footnoted in the board pack.

**Example:**  
Gross Profit: \$3,062,000  
Revenue: \$4,717,000  
**Gross Margin % = (3,062,000 ÷ 4,717,000) × 100 = 64.9%**

---

### 4. Operating Expense (OpEx)

**Business Definition:** All indirect costs incurred in running the business that are not directly attributable to loan origination or servicing. OpEx encompasses the overhead required to sustain and grow the platform, including people, technology, premises, and marketing.

**Formula:**
```
OpEx = Staff Costs + Technology & Systems Costs + Occupancy Costs
     + Marketing & Customer Acquisition Costs + General & Administrative Costs
     + Depreciation & Amortisation
```

**Data Source Fields:** `gl_account_category`, `gl_subtype`, `gl_amount`, `gl_period`, `department_code`

**Grain:** Monthly (optionally split by department or cost centre)

**Filter Rules:**
- Include only `gl_account_category = 'Operating Expense'`.
- Exclude depreciation and amortisation from OpEx when computing EBITDA (see metric 5).
- Exclude any COGS-classified items (provision, interest expense, direct servicing).
- Capital expenditure (assets on the balance sheet) must not appear in OpEx.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Finance — Financial Planning & Analysis (FP&A)

**Known Limitations:**
- Capitalisation versus expensing decisions for internally developed software are made by the Finance team under IAS 38 and can materially affect monthly OpEx. The policy is documented in the Accounting Policy Manual.

**Example:**  
Staff Costs: \$980,000 | Technology: \$210,000 | Occupancy: \$105,000  
Marketing: \$155,000 | G&A: \$88,000 | D&A: \$72,000  
**OpEx = \$1,610,000**

---

### 5. EBITDA

**Business Definition:** Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortisation. EBITDA is used as a proxy for operating cash generation, stripping out financing decisions (interest), accounting policy choices (D&A), and tax jurisdiction differences to give a comparable view of core operating performance.

**Formula:**
```
EBITDA = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses (excluding D&A)

Equivalently:
EBITDA = Net Profit + Tax Expense + Interest Expense + D&A Expense
```

**Data Source Fields:** Derived from Gross Profit, OpEx components (see metrics 2 and 4). `gl_subtype = 'Depreciation'` and `gl_subtype = 'Amortisation'` are excluded from the OpEx deduction.

**Grain:** Monthly

**Filter Rules:**
- D&A must be sourced from the fixed-asset register and confirmed against the GL `gl_subtype IN ('Depreciation', 'Amortisation')`.
- Interest on borrowings is part of COGS (cost of funds) and is already excluded from EBITDA by the Gross Profit calculation path.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Finance — FP&A

**Known Limitations:**
- For lending businesses, EBITDA has lower analytical value than for manufacturing firms because the cost of funds (interest expense) is a core operating cost, not a financing decision. EBIT or Net Interest Margin may be more meaningful for peer benchmarking.

**Example:**  
Gross Profit: \$3,062,000  
OpEx excluding D&A: \$1,538,000 (= \$1,610,000 − \$72,000 D&A)  
**EBITDA = \$3,062,000 − \$1,538,000 = \$1,524,000**  
EBITDA Margin: 32.3%

---

### 6. EBIT

**Business Definition:** Earnings Before Interest and Taxes. EBIT (also called Operating Profit) measures the profit generated purely from operations, after absorbing all operating costs including depreciation and amortisation but before the effects of capital structure (interest) and tax. It is used to assess operational efficiency independently of how the business is financed.

**Formula:**
```
EBIT = EBITDA − Depreciation & Amortisation

Equivalently:
EBIT = Gross Profit − Total OpEx (including D&A)
```

**Data Source Fields:** Derived from EBITDA and D&A (see metric 5).

**Grain:** Monthly

**Filter Rules:**
- Same as EBITDA, except D&A is now included in the OpEx deduction.
- Interest expense (cost of funds) remains in COGS and is already deducted at the Gross Profit level.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Finance — FP&A

**Known Limitations:**
- Because interest on borrowings is classified as COGS (cost of funds) rather than a below-the-line financing cost, the EBIT figure in this system differs conceptually from EBIT as defined for non-financial businesses. Users comparing to industry benchmarks should note this difference.

**Example:**  
EBITDA: \$1,524,000  
D&A: \$72,000  
**EBIT = \$1,524,000 − \$72,000 = \$1,452,000**  
EBIT Margin: 30.8%

---

### 7. Net Profit

**Business Definition:** The residual income attributable to shareholders after all revenues, costs, interest charges, and tax obligations have been accounted for. Net Profit (also called Net Income or the bottom line) is the ultimate measure of financial performance for a reporting period.

**Formula:**
```
Net Profit = EBIT − Income Tax Expense − Exceptional Items
```

**Data Source Fields:** `gl_account_category IN ('Tax Expense', 'Exceptional Items')`, plus EBIT derivation chain.

**Grain:** Monthly (reported; annualised for board presentations)

**Filter Rules:**
- Tax expense is based on the effective tax rate applied to taxable income, sourced from the Tax team's monthly accrual entry.
- Exceptional items (restructuring charges, impairment write-downs, one-off gains/losses) are reported separately and clearly labelled in the board pack.
- Exclude unrealised gains/losses on financial instruments held at fair value unless settled in the period.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Finance — Management Accounting

**Known Limitations:**
- Monthly tax expense is an estimate based on the forecast effective tax rate; the actual tax charge is finalised annually and may result in restated monthly figures.
- Exceptional items can significantly distort period-over-period comparisons. Always review the adjusted (underlying) Net Profit alongside the reported figure.

**Example:**  
EBIT: \$1,452,000  
Tax Expense (28%): \$406,560  
Exceptional Items: \$0  
**Net Profit = \$1,452,000 − \$406,560 = \$1,045,440**

---

### 8. Net Margin %

**Business Definition:** The percentage of Revenue that translates into Net Profit. Net Margin is the most comprehensive profitability ratio because it reflects all costs, including overhead, interest, and taxation. It is a key metric for investor and board reporting.

**Formula:**
```
Net Margin % = (Net Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100
```

**Data Source Fields:** Derived from Net Profit (metric 7) and Revenue (metric 1).

**Grain:** Monthly; trailing twelve months (TTM) also reported for trend analysis.

**Filter Rules:**
- If Revenue = 0, Net Margin % is undefined (`N/A`).
- Adjusted Net Margin % (excluding exceptional items) is also computed and reported alongside the statutory figure.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Finance — FP&A

**Known Limitations:**
- Net Margin % can be misleading in months with large exceptional items. The board pack always presents both the reported and adjusted (underlying) margins side by side.

**Example:**  
Net Profit: \$1,045,440  
Revenue: \$4,717,000  
**Net Margin % = (1,045,440 ÷ 4,717,000) × 100 = 22.2%**

## Budget & Variance Metrics

---

### 9. Budget Variance

**Business Definition:** The absolute monetary difference between the actual financial result and the budgeted (planned) amount for the same metric and period. A positive variance on a revenue metric means outperformance; a positive variance on a cost metric means overspend. Context determines whether the variance is favourable or unfavourable.

**Formula:**
```
Budget Variance = Actual − Budget

(For revenue/profit metrics: positive = favourable)
(For cost/expense metrics: positive = unfavourable overspend)
```

**Data Source Fields:** `actual_amount`, `budget_amount`, `metric_name`, `gl_period`, `department_code`

**Grain:** Monthly, by metric and by department/cost centre. Also rolled up to entity level.

**Filter Rules:**
- Budget figures are locked at the start of the financial year and sourced from the approved Budget file in the data layer. Mid-year re-forecasts are stored as separate `forecast_amount` fields and must not overwrite `budget_amount`.
- Variance is computed only where both `actual_amount` and `budget_amount` are non-null and non-zero for the same period and metric combination.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Finance — FP&A

**Known Limitations:**
- Budget figures were set months before the reporting period and may not reflect material market or structural changes (e.g., rapid interest-rate movements, credit deterioration). Variance commentary must distinguish between structural change and operational performance.

**Example:**  
Actual Revenue: \$4,717,000 | Budget Revenue: \$4,500,000  
**Budget Variance = \$4,717,000 − \$4,500,000 = +\$217,000 (Favourable)**

---

### 10. Variance Percentage

**Business Definition:** The Budget Variance expressed as a percentage of the budgeted amount. It normalises the variance so that variances across metrics with very different absolute scales (e.g., Revenue vs. a small cost line) can be compared and prioritised consistently.

**Formula:**
```
Variance % = (Budget Variance ÷ Budget Amount) × 100
           = ((Actual − Budget) ÷ Budget) × 100
```

**Data Source Fields:** Derived from `actual_amount` and `budget_amount`.

**Grain:** Monthly, by metric and department.

**Filter Rules:**
- If `budget_amount = 0`, Variance % is undefined (`N/A`).
- Thresholds for escalation: `|Variance %| ≥ 5%` triggers an automatic commentary flag in the board pack; `|Variance %| ≥ 10%` triggers a formal variance analysis note.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Finance — FP&A

**Known Limitations:**
- Percentage variance on very small budget amounts (e.g., a cost line budgeted at \$10,000) can produce very large percentages that are operationally insignificant. Always review in conjunction with the absolute variance amount.

**Example:**  
Budget Variance: +\$217,000 | Budget Amount: \$4,500,000  
**Variance % = (217,000 ÷ 4,500,000) × 100 = +4.8% (Favourable)**

---

### 11. Favorable Variance

**Business Definition:** A budget variance is classified as **Favourable** when the actual result is better than budget from a profitability perspective: actual revenue exceeds budget, or actual cost is below budget.

**Formula:**
```
Favorable Variance flag:
  For income/profit metrics: Actual > Budget → Favourable = TRUE
  For cost/expense metrics:  Actual < Budget → Favourable = TRUE
```

**Data Source Fields:** `actual_amount`, `budget_amount`, `metric_type` (`'income'` or `'expense'`)

**Grain:** Monthly, per metric line.

**Filter Rules:**
- The `metric_type` classification (`income` vs. `expense`) is defined in the metric registry within `config.yaml` and must not be inferred ad hoc.
- Exactly zero variance (Actual = Budget) is classified as `On Budget`, not Favourable.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Finance — FP&A

**Known Limitations:**
- A favourable revenue variance driven by higher-risk loan originations may not represent true outperformance if it is accompanied by a deterioration in credit quality. Variance analysis must always be read alongside the credit risk metrics.

**Example:**  
Actual OpEx: \$1,560,000 | Budget OpEx: \$1,610,000  
Actual < Budget for a cost metric → **Favourable Variance = TRUE (+\$50,000)**

---

### 12. Unfavorable Variance

**Business Definition:** A budget variance is classified as **Unfavourable** when the actual result is worse than budget from a profitability perspective: actual revenue is below budget, or actual cost exceeds budget.

**Formula:**
```
Unfavorable Variance flag:
  For income/profit metrics: Actual < Budget → Unfavourable = TRUE
  For cost/expense metrics:  Actual > Budget → Unfavourable = TRUE
```

**Data Source Fields:** `actual_amount`, `budget_amount`, `metric_type`

**Grain:** Monthly, per metric line.

**Filter Rules:**
- Same `metric_type` classification rules as Favourable Variance (metric 11).
- All unfavourable variances above the materiality threshold (\$50,000 absolute or 5% relative) must have a written commentary note in the board pack.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Finance — FP&A

**Known Limitations:**
- Unfavourable variances arising from deliberate strategic decisions (e.g., investing in a new technology platform beyond budget) are qualitatively different from operational underperformance. The variance commentary system must distinguish between these cases.

**Example:**  
Actual Revenue: \$4,200,000 | Budget Revenue: \$4,500,000  
Actual < Budget for an income metric → **Unfavourable Variance = TRUE (−\$300,000)**

## Credit Risk Ratios

---

### 13. DTI Ratio (Debt-to-Income)

**Business Definition:** The Debt-to-Income ratio measures the proportion of a borrower's gross monthly income that is consumed by existing debt obligations. It is one of the most important underwriting signals in consumer lending, as high DTI is strongly associated with repayment stress and default.

**Formula:**
```
DTI Ratio (%) = (Total Monthly Debt Obligations ÷ Gross Monthly Income) × 100
```

Where total monthly debt obligations include the loan instalment being applied for, plus all other recurring debt payments reported by the borrower or pulled from the credit bureau.

**Data Source Fields:** `dti` (Lending Club field, pre-computed), `annual_inc`, `installment`, `loan_amnt`

**Grain:** Loan-level (at origination)

**Filter Rules:**
- Use the Lending Club `dti` field directly; it already incorporates all bureau-reported obligations.
- Exclude loans where `dti IS NULL` or `dti < 0` (data quality exceptions).
- Exclude loans with `annual_inc = 0` or `annual_inc IS NULL`.
- For segmentation analysis, use DTI bands: `<20%`, `20–35%`, `35–45%`, `>45%`.

**Refresh Frequency:** Static at origination; refreshed monthly for portfolio-level distribution analysis.

**Owner:** Risk — Credit Risk Analytics

**Known Limitations:**
- DTI is self-reported income and bureau-reported debt; income is not independently verified in the Lending Club dataset. Income inflation by borrowers may cause DTI to be understated.
- DTI does not capture irregular income sources (self-employed, gig workers) accurately.

**Example:**  
Borrower monthly debt payments: \$1,800 | Gross monthly income: \$6,000  
**DTI = (1,800 ÷ 6,000) × 100 = 30.0%**  
Classification: Moderate risk band (20–35%)

---

### 14. Revolving Utilization Rate

**Business Definition:** The proportion of a borrower's total revolving credit limit (credit cards, lines of credit) that is currently drawn. High revolving utilisation is a strong indicator of financial stress and correlates with elevated default risk; it is one of the highest-weighted components in consumer credit scores.

**Formula:**
```
Revolving Utilization Rate (%) = (Total Revolving Balance ÷ Total Revolving Credit Limit) × 100
```

**Data Source Fields:** `revol_util` (Lending Club field, pre-computed as a percentage string — must be parsed to float), `revol_bal`

**Grain:** Loan-level (at origination)

**Filter Rules:**
- Parse `revol_util` from string format (e.g., `"35.6%"` → `0.356`) during data ingestion.
- Exclude records where `revol_util > 100%` (data anomaly — capped at 100 for modelling unless under investigation).
- Exclude records where `revol_util IS NULL`.
- Segmentation bands: `<30%` (low), `30–60%` (moderate), `60–80%` (high), `>80%` (very high).

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly portfolio distribution; static at origination for scorecard.

**Owner:** Risk — Credit Risk Analytics

**Known Limitations:**
- Revolving utilisation reflects a snapshot at loan origination, not the ongoing balance. Borrowers who pay down cards immediately after origination (a form of gaming) may appear lower-risk than they are.

**Example:**  
Revolving balance: \$8,500 | Revolving credit limit: \$20,000  
**Revolving Utilization Rate = (8,500 ÷ 20,000) × 100 = 42.5%** (Moderate band)

---

### 15. Delinquency Rate

**Business Definition:** The proportion of the active loan portfolio (by count or outstanding balance) that is past due by one or more payment periods but has not yet been classified as defaulted. Delinquency is an early-warning indicator of credit quality deterioration and a leading signal for expected loss provisioning.

**Formula:**
```
Delinquency Rate (%) = (Number of Loans 30+ Days Past Due ÷ Total Active Loans) × 100

Balance-weighted variant:
Delinquency Rate (%) = (Outstanding Balance of Loans 30+ DPD ÷ Total Outstanding Balance) × 100
```

**Data Source Fields:** `loan_status`, `out_prncp`, `loan_amnt`, `last_pymnt_d`, `next_pymnt_d`, `gl_period`

**Grain:** Monthly portfolio snapshot

**Filter Rules:**
- Include only loans where `loan_status IN ('Late (16-30 days)', 'Late (31-120 days)', 'In Grace Period')`.
- Exclude fully charged-off or defaulted loans (captured in Default Rate, metric 16).
- Exclude fully paid loans.
- Report at three bucket levels: 1–29 DPD (grace period / early delinquency), 30–59 DPD, 60–89 DPD, 90+ DPD.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Risk — Portfolio Risk Management

**Known Limitations:**
- The Lending Club dataset represents a snapshot of loan status at a point in time and does not contain a full daily payment history. The delinquency bucket assignment relies on the `loan_status` field, which may lag actual payment behaviour by up to one month.

**Example:**  
Active loans: 15,420 | Loans 30+ DPD: 924  
**Delinquency Rate = (924 ÷ 15,420) × 100 = 5.99%**

### 16. Default Rate

**Business Definition:** The proportion of the loan portfolio that has been classified as defaulted (Charged Off) during a given period or cumulatively over the life of a vintage cohort. The default rate is the primary outcome measure for credit quality and is the basis for ECL provisioning under IFRS 9.

**Formula:**
```
Period Default Rate (%) = (Loans Newly Defaulted in Period ÷ Active Loans at Start of Period) × 100

Cumulative Default Rate (%) = (Total Loans Defaulted to Date ÷ Total Loans Originated in Cohort) × 100
```

**Data Source Fields:** `loan_status`, `out_prncp`, `loan_amnt`, `issue_d` (origination date), `last_pymnt_d`, `charged_off_date` (derived)

**Grain:** Monthly (period rate); vintage-level (cumulative rate)

**Filter Rules:**
- Default flag: `loan_status IN ('Charged Off', 'Default')` → `is_default = 1`.
- Exclude loans with `loan_status = 'Does not meet the credit policy. Status: Charged Off'` from primary default rate (count separately).
- Current + Fully Paid = performing. All else = excluded from active denominator.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Risk — Credit Risk Analytics

**Known Limitations:**
- Charged-off timing in the dataset depends on lender charge-off policy, which varied across the Lending Club historical period. Early vintages (2007–2010) used different charge-off timing rules than post-2015 vintages, affecting comparability.

**Example:**  
Active loans at start of October 2024: 15,420  
Newly charged off in October 2024: 231  
**Default Rate = (231 ÷ 15,420) × 100 = 1.50%**

## Scorecard Diagnostics

---

### 17. Probability of Default (PD)

**Business Definition:** The statistical probability that a borrower will default on their loan within a defined time horizon (typically 12 months). PD is the output of the credit scorecard model and is used as the primary risk signal for loan origination decisions, risk-based pricing, and IFRS 9 expected credit loss provisioning.

**Formula:**
```
PD = P(Default) = 1 ÷ (1 + e^(−z))

Where: z = β₀ + β₁X₁ + β₂X₂ + ... + βₙXₙ

X₁..Xₙ = WOE-transformed input features
β₀..βₙ = logistic regression coefficients estimated on training data
```

**Data Source Fields:** WOE-transformed features: `dti_woe`, `revol_util_woe`, `annual_inc_woe`, `fico_range_woe`, `emp_length_woe`, `purpose_woe`, `home_ownership_woe`

**Grain:** Loan-level (at origination); also computed monthly for portfolio monitoring

**Filter Rules:**
- PD is computed only for loans with complete feature data (no nulls in input features after WOE imputation).
- PD values are bounded to `[0.001, 0.999]` to avoid log(0) issues in downstream EL calculations.
- The model is trained on loans with a resolved outcome (Fully Paid or Charged Off); `Current` and `Late` loans are excluded from training.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly portfolio scoring; model retrained annually or upon significant population stability index (PSI) breach.

**Owner:** Risk — Model Risk Management

**Known Limitations:**
- The logistic regression model assumes a linear relationship between WOE-transformed features and log-odds of default, which may not hold at the extremes of the feature distributions.
- PD estimates are point-in-time and do not explicitly incorporate macroeconomic conditioning required for IFRS 9 through-the-cycle PD.

**Example:**  
Borrower with DTI=35%, revol_util=60%, FICO=680: z = −1.42  
**PD = 1 ÷ (1 + e^(1.42)) = 1 ÷ (1 + 4.137) = 0.195 = 19.5%**

### 18. Weight of Evidence (WOE)

**Business Definition:** A transformation applied to each predictor variable that expresses the log-odds of a good outcome (non-default) relative to a bad outcome (default) for each bin of that variable. WOE transforms raw variable values into a scale that is directly proportional to the log-odds of the target, making them suitable inputs for logistic regression.

**Formula:**
```
WOE_i = ln(Distribution of Events_i ÷ Distribution of Non-Events_i)
       = ln((Bads_i / Total_Bads) ÷ (Goods_i / Total_Goods))

Where:
  Bads_i  = number of defaulters in bin i
  Goods_i = number of non-defaulters in bin i
  Total_Bads  = total defaulters in the dataset
  Total_Goods = total non-defaulters in the dataset
```

**Data Source Fields:** Binned predictor variable (e.g., `dti_bin`), `is_default` target flag

**Grain:** Bin-level (each bin of each predictor variable produces one WOE value)

**Filter Rules:**
- Each bin must contain at least 5% of the total population and at least 50 observations to ensure statistical stability.
- If any bin contains 0 bads or 0 goods, apply a smoothing constant of 0.5 before computing WOE.
- WOE should exhibit monotonic ordering (consistently increasing or decreasing across ordered bins) for scorecard interpretability; non-monotonic bins are merged.

**Refresh Frequency:** Recalculated when model is retrained (annually or on PSI breach).

**Owner:** Risk — Model Risk Management

**Known Limitations:**
- WOE binning can mask non-linear relationships within bins. The choice of bin boundaries materially affects the WOE values and downstream Information Value.
- WOE is undefined for empty bins; the smoothing constant introduces a minor bias.

**Example:**  
DTI bin `35–45%`: Bads = 420, Goods = 1,080  
Total Bads = 3,200, Total Goods = 11,800  
Distribution of Events = 420/3,200 = 0.1313  
Distribution of Non-Events = 1,080/11,800 = 0.0915  
**WOE = ln(0.1313 ÷ 0.0915) = ln(1.435) = +0.361**

---

### 19. Information Value (IV)

**Business Definition:** Information Value quantifies the overall predictive power of a single variable by aggregating the WOE differences across all its bins, weighted by the spread between event and non-event distributions. IV provides a single number that ranks variables by their discriminatory ability and guides variable selection for the scorecard.

**Formula:**
```
IV = Σᵢ [(Distribution of Events_i − Distribution of Non-Events_i) × WOE_i]

IV Interpretation:
  < 0.02  → Useless predictor
  0.02–0.1 → Weak predictor
  0.1–0.3 → Medium predictor (include in scorecard)
  0.3–0.5 → Strong predictor (include in scorecard)
  > 0.5   → Suspiciously strong (check for data leakage)
```

**Data Source Fields:** WOE values per bin (see metric 18), `is_default` target flag.

**Grain:** Variable-level (one IV value per predictor variable)

**Filter Rules:**
- Variables with IV < 0.02 are excluded from the scorecard after variable selection review.
- Variables with IV > 0.5 are flagged for data-leakage investigation before inclusion.
- IV is computed on the training dataset only; out-of-sample IV on the validation set must be within 20% of training IV, otherwise overfitting is suspected.

**Refresh Frequency:** Annual model rebuild or on PSI breach.

**Owner:** Risk — Model Risk Management

**Known Limitations:**
- IV does not account for correlations between variables. Two variables with IV=0.3 each may carry redundant information if highly correlated, reducing their combined contribution below 0.6.

**Example:**  
DTI variable, 5 bins:  

| Bin | Dist. Events | Dist. Non-Events | WOE | (Events−NonEvents)×WOE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| <20% | 0.0812 | 0.1532 | −0.634 | +0.0456 |
| 20–35% | 0.2250 | 0.3105 | −0.323 | +0.0276 |
| 35–45% | 0.1313 | 0.0915 | +0.361 | +0.0144 |
| 45–60% | 0.3094 | 0.2814 | +0.095 | +0.0027 |
| >60% | 0.2531 | 0.1634 | +0.436 | +0.0391 |  
**IV(DTI) = 0.1294 → Medium predictor**

### 20. Gini Coefficient

**Business Definition:** The Gini Coefficient (also called the Accuracy Ratio) measures the overall discriminatory power of the PD model. It quantifies how well the model separates defaulters from non-defaulters across the full score distribution. A Gini of 0 means the model performs no better than random; a Gini of 1 means perfect separation.

**Formula:**
```
Gini = 2 × AUROC − 1

Where AUROC = Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve
            = ∫₀¹ TPR d(FPR)
```

**Data Source Fields:** Predicted `pd_score` (model output), `is_default` (actual outcome), on the held-out test set.

**Grain:** Model-level (one value per model validation exercise)

**Filter Rules:**
- Computed on the out-of-sample test set only (never on training data).
- Minimum acceptable Gini for a production scorecard: **0.35**. Values below this threshold trigger model review.
- Gini must be recomputed on the most recent 12 months of originated-and-resolved loans at each annual validation cycle.

**Refresh Frequency:** Annual model validation; monitored monthly via performance tracking.

**Owner:** Risk — Model Risk Management

**Known Limitations:**
- Gini (AUROC) is insensitive to calibration: a model can have a high Gini but systematically over- or under-estimate PD. Always review Gini alongside a calibration plot (observed vs. predicted default rates by score decile).

**Example:**  
Test set: 3,000 loans (600 defaulters, 2,400 non-defaulters)  
AUROC computed via trapezoidal integration of the ROC curve: 0.74  
**Gini = 2 × 0.74 − 1 = 0.48** → Strong discriminatory power

---

### 21. KS Statistic

**Business Definition:** The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) Statistic measures the maximum separation between the cumulative distribution of defaulters and the cumulative distribution of non-defaulters across all score thresholds. It identifies the score point at which the model most powerfully distinguishes the two populations and provides an alternative discriminatory power measure to the Gini.

**Formula:**
```
KS = max|CDF_Bads(s) − CDF_Goods(s)| over all score thresholds s

Where:
  CDF_Bads(s)  = cumulative proportion of defaulters with score ≤ s
  CDF_Goods(s) = cumulative proportion of non-defaulters with score ≤ s
```

**Data Source Fields:** Predicted `pd_score`, `is_default`, on the test set.

**Grain:** Model-level

**Filter Rules:**
- KS is computed on the out-of-sample test set.
- Minimum acceptable KS: **25%** for a production scorecard. Values below this threshold require model review.
- Report both the KS value and the score decile at which it occurs (the optimal cut-off point).

**Refresh Frequency:** Annual validation; monthly monitoring.

**Owner:** Risk — Model Risk Management

**Known Limitations:**
- KS focuses on the single point of maximum separation and ignores discriminatory power across the rest of the score distribution. A model can have a high KS in a narrow score range while performing poorly elsewhere. Always use KS alongside Gini for a complete picture.

**Example:**  
Score decile analysis:  
At score decile 4 (cumulative): CDF_Bads = 0.68, CDF_Goods = 0.29  
Separation = 0.68 − 0.29 = 0.39  
**KS = 39.0%** → Acceptable discriminatory power

---

### 22. Risk Band

**Business Definition:** A categorical label assigned to each loan application based on its PD score, used to operationalise credit decisions at the point of origination and to segment the portfolio for monitoring and reporting. Risk bands provide a business-friendly summary of the continuous PD output.

**Formula:**
```
Risk Band = f(PD Score):
  PD ≤ 5%        → "A — Very Low Risk"
  5% < PD ≤ 10%  → "B — Low Risk"
  10% < PD ≤ 20% → "C — Medium Risk"
  20% < PD ≤ 35% → "D — High Risk"
  PD > 35%       → "E — Very High Risk"
```

**Data Source Fields:** `pd_score` (model output)

**Grain:** Loan-level; also aggregated to portfolio-level band distribution (% of book by count and balance in each band).

**Filter Rules:**
- Band boundaries are defined in `config.yaml` and must not be changed without formal model risk review.
- Loans without a PD score (missing features) are assigned band `U — Unscored` and treated as high risk for provisioning purposes.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly portfolio band distribution report.

**Owner:** Risk — Credit Risk Analytics

**Known Limitations:**
- Rigid band boundaries can create cliff-effects: two loans with PD=19.9% and PD=20.1% are treated very differently at the medium/high boundary despite near-identical underlying risk.

**Example:**  
Loan A: PD = 8.3% → **Band B — Low Risk** (auto-approve with standard pricing)  
Loan B: PD = 24.1% → **Band D — High Risk** (manual review required)

## Expected Loss Components

### 23. Expected Loss (EL)

**Business Definition:** Expected Loss is the statistically expected monetary loss from a loan or portfolio over a defined time horizon. It is the product of three components: how likely default is (PD), how much is owed at the moment of default (EAD), and how much of that exposure cannot be recovered (LGD). EL is used for IFRS 9 ECL provisioning and risk-adjusted return calculations.

**Formula:**
```
Expected Loss (EL) = PD × EAD × LGD

Expressed in monetary terms:
  EL ($) = PD (decimal) × EAD ($) × LGD (decimal)

As a rate:
  EL (%) = PD (%) × LGD (%)
```

**Data Source Fields:** `pd_score`, `out_prncp` (outstanding principal), `funded_amnt`, `recoveries`, `total_rec_prncp`

**Grain:** Loan-level; aggregated to portfolio level and by risk band.

**Filter Rules:**
- Use 12-month PD for Stage 1 loans (IFRS 9); lifetime PD for Stage 2 and Stage 3 loans.
- EAD = current outstanding principal + 12-month projected accrued interest (simplified approach).
- LGD is computed as portfolio-level average from historical recoveries (see metric 25).

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly (drives the provision for credit losses GL entry).

**Owner:** Risk — Credit Risk Analytics; Finance — Management Accounting (for provisioning)

**Known Limitations:**
- EL is a statistical expectation, not a worst-case loss. Actual losses can materially exceed EL in stress scenarios, which is why the Risk team also computes Unexpected Loss (UL) using Value-at-Risk or stress testing.

**Example:**  
Loan: PD = 15%, EAD = \$18,500, LGD = 55%  
**EL = 0.15 × \$18,500 × 0.55 = \$1,526.25**  
Portfolio EL rate: 15% × 55% = **8.25%**

---

### 24. Exposure at Default (EAD)

**Business Definition:** The estimated outstanding loan balance at the point of default — i.e., how much the institution is owed if the borrower stops paying today. For term loans, EAD is primarily the outstanding principal plus accrued interest; for revolving facilities, it also includes undrawn commitments that the borrower may draw before defaulting.

**Formula:**
```
EAD (Term Loan) = Outstanding Principal + Accrued Interest Receivable
                = out_prncp + (annual_int_rate × out_prncp × DPD/365)

EAD (Simplified, monthly reporting):
  EAD ≈ out_prncp  (accrued interest approximated as immaterial for 30-day horizon)
```

**Data Source Fields:** `out_prncp`, `int_rate`, `installment`, `loan_amnt`, `total_pymnt`

**Grain:** Loan-level (computed at each monthly reporting date)

**Filter Rules:**
- EAD must be non-negative. If `out_prncp` is 0 or negative (fully paid), EAD = 0 and the loan is excluded from EL calculations.
- For charged-off loans, EAD is set to the balance at charge-off (for LGD calculation); do not use the current `out_prncp` field.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Risk — Credit Risk Analytics

**Known Limitations:**
- The simplified EAD formula ignores credit conversion factors (CCFs) for any undrawn revolving credit component. For a pure term-loan portfolio (Lending Club), this simplification is acceptable; for a mixed portfolio including credit lines, a CCF model is required.

**Example:**  
Loan outstanding principal: \$18,200  
Accrued interest (15 days at 14.5% p.a.): \$18,200 × 0.145 × 15/365 = \$108.19  
**EAD = \$18,200 + \$108 = \$18,308**

---

### 25. Loss Given Default (LGD)

**Business Definition:** The proportion of the Exposure at Default that is ultimately lost after all recovery efforts (collections, legal action, asset liquidation) have been exhausted. LGD = 1 − Recovery Rate. For unsecured consumer loans, LGD is typically high (45–80%) because there is no collateral to recover against.

**Formula:**
```
LGD = 1 − Recovery Rate
Recovery Rate = Total Recovered Amount ÷ EAD at Default

Total Recovered Amount = recoveries + (recovery_coll_fee adjusted)
```

**Data Source Fields:** `recoveries`, `collection_recovery_fee`, `out_prncp` (at charge-off), `funded_amnt`, `total_rec_prncp`

**Grain:** Loan-level (for resolved charged-off loans); portfolio average (for provisioning).

**Filter Rules:**
- LGD is computed only on charged-off loans with `loan_status = 'Charged Off'` and where the charge-off date is at least 24 months in the past (to allow recovery proceedings to mature).
- Net recoveries = `recoveries − collection_recovery_fee` (net of collection costs).
- LGD must be bounded: `0 ≤ LGD ≤ 1`. If net recoveries exceed the charge-off balance, cap LGD at 0.

**Refresh Frequency:** Annual recalculation from resolved cohorts; portfolio average applied monthly.

**Owner:** Risk — Credit Risk Analytics

**Known Limitations:**
- Recovery amounts in the Lending Club dataset are as reported and may not fully reflect the multi-year recovery process for all charged-off accounts. LGD estimates may be understated if late recoveries are not fully captured.

**Example:**  
Charged-off loan EAD: \$12,400  
Gross recoveries: \$3,100 | Collection fees: \$620  
Net recoveries: \$2,480  
Recovery Rate = \$2,480 ÷ \$12,400 = 20.0%  
**LGD = 1 − 0.20 = 80.0%**

### 26. Vintage Analysis

**Business Definition:** Vintage Analysis tracks the cumulative default rate (or other performance metrics) of cohorts of loans grouped by their origination period (the "vintage"), as those loans age over time. It is the primary tool for detecting systematic changes in underwriting standards, economic cycles, or portfolio mix over time.

**Formula:**
```
Cumulative Default Rate (Vintage v, Month-on-Book m):
  CDR(v, m) = Cumulative Defaulted Balance in Vintage v up to Month m
              ÷ Original Balance Originated in Vintage v
            × 100

Month-on-Book (MoB) = months since the vintage origination date
```

**Data Source Fields:** `issue_d` (origination month → vintage), `loan_amnt`, `is_default`, `charged_off_date` (derived from `last_pymnt_d` and `loan_status`), `out_prncp`

**Grain:** Vintage × Month-on-Book (produces a matrix: rows = vintage quarters, columns = MoB 1–60)

**Filter Rules:**
- Vintages are defined at monthly or quarterly granularity (quarterly preferred for sufficient volume).
- Only include vintages with at least 100 loans originated.
- Restrict the vintage matrix to MoB 1–60 (5 years) for 36-month loans; MoB 1–84 for 60-month loans.
- Treat `loan_status IN ('Charged Off', 'Default')` as defaulted; compute cumulative from origination month.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly (each month adds one new MoB column to all active vintages).

**Owner:** Risk — Portfolio Risk Management

**Known Limitations:**
- Vintage analysis does not control for macroeconomic conditions. A vintage with high CDR may reflect poor underwriting, an economic downturn, or both. Macro-adjusted vintage analysis requires overlaying GDP, unemployment, or credit spread indices.

**Example:**  
Q1 2016 Vintage: 2,450 loans, \$38.5M originated  
At MoB 12: 98 defaults → CDR = 2.5%  
At MoB 24: 245 defaults → CDR = 6.4%  
At MoB 36: 382 defaults → CDR = 9.9%  
At MoB 48: 441 defaults → CDR = 11.5% (plateau — most defaults occur in years 1–3)

### 27. Roll Rate

**Business Definition:** Roll Rate measures the probability that a loan in one delinquency bucket (e.g., 30–59 DPD) will transition to a worse bucket (e.g., 60–89 DPD), remain in the same bucket, or cure (return to current) in the next period. Roll rate matrices are used for loss forecasting, reserve adequacy testing, and understanding the dynamics of portfolio deterioration.

**Formula:**
```
Roll Rate(from bucket A → bucket B) = 
  Number of Loans in Bucket A in Month t that are in Bucket B in Month t+1
  ÷ Total Number of Loans in Bucket A in Month t
  × 100

Bucket definitions:
  Current (C): 0 DPD
  Early Delinquency (E): 1–29 DPD
  30 DPD: 30–59 DPD
  60 DPD: 60–89 DPD
  90+ DPD: 90+ DPD
  Default (D): Charged Off / Default
  Paid Off (P): Fully Paid
```

**Data Source Fields:** `loan_status` at consecutive monthly snapshots (requires a longitudinal panel dataset built from monthly snapshots), `loan_id`, `gl_period`

**Grain:** Transition-level (from bucket × to bucket × in month t → t+1); aggregated to roll rate matrix.

**Filter Rules:**
- Build a monthly panel dataset with one row per `(loan_id, month)` to track transitions.
- The Lending Club dataset does not include full monthly snapshots; transitions are approximated from `loan_status` at the observation date and historical payment data.
- Roll rates should be computed on a minimum of 6 months of history and smoothed as a 3-month rolling average to reduce noise.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Risk — Portfolio Risk Management

**Known Limitations:**
- Because the Lending Club public dataset is a cross-sectional snapshot rather than a full monthly panel, roll rates in this system are approximated rather than computed from true month-to-month transitions. For production use, integrate with the core banking system's daily payment ledger.

**Example Roll Rate Matrix (illustrative):**

| From\To | Current | 30 DPD | 60 DPD | 90+ DPD | Default | Paid Off |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Current | 92.1% | 4.2% | 0.3% | 0.1% | 0.1% | 3.2% |
| 30 DPD | 38.5% | 42.3% | 15.6% | 1.8% | 0.4% | 1.4% |
| 60 DPD | 18.2% | 12.4% | 38.1% | 28.6% | 1.9% | 0.8% |
| 90+ DPD | 8.1% | 5.3% | 10.2% | 54.4% | 20.6% | 1.4% |

**Interpretation:** 38.5% of loans in 30 DPD cure to Current the following month; 15.6% roll forward to 60 DPD.

### 28. Concentration Risk

**Business Definition:** Concentration Risk quantifies the degree to which a portfolio's credit exposure is concentrated in a small number of borrowers, geographies, industries, loan purposes, or risk segments. High concentration amplifies the impact of an adverse event in that segment and is a key concern for portfolio diversification, regulatory capital adequacy, and board-level risk appetite.

**Formula:**
```
Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) — the primary concentration measure:

HHI = Σᵢ (sᵢ)²

Where:
  sᵢ = share of total outstanding balance in segment i (as a decimal, 0–1)
  Σᵢ sᵢ = 1

HHI ranges from 1/N (perfectly diversified across N segments)
         to 1.0 (100% concentrated in one segment)

Normalised HHI:
  HHI* = (HHI − 1/N) ÷ (1 − 1/N)  → ranges from 0 (perfect diversification) to 1 (monopoly)

Top-N Concentration:
  Top-5 Concentration = sum of balance shares of the 5 largest segments × 100%
```

**Data Source Fields:** `loan_amnt`, `out_prncp`, `purpose`, `addr_state`, `grade`, `risk_band`, `emp_industry` (where available)

**Grain:** Portfolio-level; computed separately for each segmentation dimension (purpose, geography, risk band, term).

**Filter Rules:**
- Compute HHI on current outstanding balance (`out_prncp`), not original loan amount.
- Exclude fully paid and charged-off loans (EAD = 0).
- Minimum segment count for HHI: at least 5 non-zero segments; otherwise, report "Insufficient diversification" rather than a spuriously low HHI.
- Thresholds for concentration alerts: `HHI* > 0.25` → Moderate concentration flag; `HHI* > 0.50` → High concentration — board escalation required.

**Refresh Frequency:** Monthly

**Owner:** Risk — Portfolio Risk Management

**Known Limitations:**
- HHI treats all segments as equally risky, which is not the case. A portfolio concentrated in Band A (very low risk) loans is less concerning than one concentrated in Band D (high risk) loans with the same HHI. Always review concentration metrics alongside risk-band distribution.
- Geographic concentration analysis relies on `addr_state`, which is state-level only. For institutions with significant sub-state regional variation (e.g., rural vs. urban exposure), state-level aggregation may mask meaningful local concentrations.

**Example:**  
Portfolio by loan purpose (outstanding balance):  
Debt Consolidation: 48% | Credit Card: 22% | Home Improvement: 12%  
Small Business: 8% | Medical: 5% | Other: 5%  

HHI = 0.48² + 0.22² + 0.12² + 0.08² + 0.05² + 0.05²  
    = 0.2304 + 0.0484 + 0.0144 + 0.0064 + 0.0025 + 0.0025  
    = **0.3046**  
Normalised HHI* = (0.3046 − 1/6) ÷ (1 − 1/6) = (0.3046 − 0.1667) ÷ 0.8333 = **0.166**  
→ Moderate diversification; Debt Consolidation concentration should be monitored.
